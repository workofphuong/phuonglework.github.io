Bearcat Venture

Seeing startups from the investor side

Student-led venture fund, University of Cincinnati.

As a fund analyst I judged startups' go-to-market plans the way an investor does, and that research informed funding decisions for 4 early-stage startups.

The problem

An investor has to decide whether a startup can reach its market, not only whether the product works. My job was to answer that with research.

What I did

1. Size the market. I used PitchBook and Excel to calculate total addressable market, evaluate financial performance and model company valuations.

2. Find the white space. I mapped industry trends against scalability to find where competitors were thin.

3. Test the go-to-market. I evaluated each startup's go-to-market strategy through market research, customer insight analysis and competitive benchmarking. I turned the competitive research into product "battle cards" to judge whether a new launch could enter its market.

One example, with the names removed

I can't name the startups or share what the fund decided, so here is the shape of the work for one US biosensor company, using figures that are public. I sized the market from the top down: a 40B total market for US biosensors, narrowing to 26.4B in medical segments and 2.6B among early adopters, the first customers a young company can reach.

Then I benchmarked five comparable biosensor and medtech companies on funding. The median round was $10.5M and the median post-valuation was $32.43M. These companies typically took several years to reach their first Series A.

What it taught me about marketing

I learned to read a market before choosing a launch strategy. When a market is young, a product can compete on low price; when it is mature, differentiation is often the only option. I now check market maturity before I choose a message.
